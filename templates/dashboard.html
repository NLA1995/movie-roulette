Fifty Years of Genre Taste: What Changes When You Normalise

Data source: TMDB /discover counts of films with 50+ votes, by genre and decade (1970s–2020s). Default view: each genre's share of all films released in that decade. A film is counted in every genre it is tagged with, so a decade's shares add up to more than 100%.

Why Normalise?

In raw counts, almost every genre "grows" simply because the industry grows: the number of films in this dataset rises from 1,795 in the 1970s to 12,173 in the 2010s — nearly seven times more. A raw-count heatmap therefore mostly shows one story (more films every decade) and hides the more interesting one: which genres took a bigger or smaller slice of the pie.

The default view divides each genre's count by the total number of films in that decade. Darker cells mean the genre made up a larger share of that decade's output. This also solves the incomplete 2020s: a share is comparable even when the decade is only partly over. The Film count button switches back to raw volumes.

What the Shares Reveal

Drama — Constant, Not Growing

Drama is the darkest row in every decade, the largest genre by a clear margin. But its share is roughly stable over fifty years. In raw counts Drama looks like it "exploded" in the 2010s; normalised, it simply kept its slice while the whole pie grew. Part of this is taxonomy: Drama is the broadest label on TMDB, attached to anything with serious emotional stakes.

Comedy — A Peak in the 1980s–90s

Comedy is the clear second genre throughout, with its strongest share in the 1980s and 1990s and a gradual decline since. Its slice is smaller in the 2010s and 2020s than at its peak.

Romance — The 1990s–2000s Bump

Romance shows the most distinctive arc on the chart: its share rises noticeably in the 1990s and 2000s, then falls back, to 13.3% of films in the 2020s. This lines up with the era of the mid-budget theatrical romantic comedy, a format that largely disappeared from cinemas later on.

Horror — Strongest in the 1970s–80s

Horror took its largest slice in the 1970s and 1980s, the slasher era, then dropped sharply in the 1990s. It has partially recovered since, but not to its earlier share. Popular narratives of a 2010s "horror renaissance" are about visibility and box office, not about horror becoming a larger share of what gets made.

Thriller and Action — Steady Middle

Thriller and Action sit in a similar mid-range band across all decades. Thriller's share is highest in the 2020s column, the clearest recent riser on the chart.

Sci-Fi, Fantasy, Animation, Documentary — The Long Tail

These four genres each hold a small share in every decade. Documentary is the smallest row throughout, partly because documentaries attract fewer votes on TMDB and the 50-vote threshold filters many of them out. Their cultural visibility (franchises, streaming hits) is far larger than their share of total output.

Limitations

  • Vote threshold bias: only films with 50+ TMDB votes are counted. That favours films with an online, mostly English-speaking audience, and disproportionately drops older, non-Western and niche titles, documentaries in particular.
  • Multi-genre tagging: a film tagged Action + Thriller + Sci-Fi counts in all three. Shares overlap, and genres that are usually secondary tags (Thriller, Action) can look larger than their "primary genre" share would be.
  • TMDB's taxonomy: genre labels are crowd-maintained and applied inconsistently across eras.
  • Output, not success: a large share means many films were made, not that they were good, popular or profitable. See the ROI chart below for the money side.

Conclusion

Normalising turns a chart about industry growth into a chart about taste. Drama and Comedy dominate every decade, but the movement is elsewhere: Horror's share peaked early and never fully returned, Romance had a distinct 1990s–2000s moment, and Thriller is the genre gaining ground most recently. The open questions are good ones to explore next: does the Romance decline reflect audiences or distribution, and would the picture change if only each film's primary genre were counted?

Does Money Buy Quality? What 76 Popular Films Say

Data source: popular films with 1,000+ TMDB votes and a reported budget. X-axis: production budget in millions of 2025 USD (inflation-adjusted with US CPI-U), log scale. Y-axis: TMDB audience rating. Dot color: total vote count (purple = under 10k, yellow = 40k+).

How to Read the Chart

Each dot is one film: further right means more expensive, higher means better rated. Budgets are converted to 2025 dollars so a 1970s production and a 2020s one are comparable, and plotted on a log scale because budgets are heavily skewed: in this sample they run from under $5M to around $500M, with most films above $100M. On a log axis, each gridline step is a multiplication, so a $10M film and a $100M film are as far apart as a $100M film and a $1B one.

The Statistics

  • Spearman ρ = +0.11, p = 0.328. Spearman's rank correlation asks a simple question: do more expensive films tend to rank higher in rating? A value of +0.11 is a very weak positive tendency, and the permutation test (shuffling the ratings 2,000 times) shows a correlation this strong appears by chance about a third of the time. Conclusion: no statistically significant association.
  • R² = 0.088 for the dashed trend line, a linear regression of rating on log budget. Budget "explains" under 9% of the variation in ratings.

Why does the trend line slope upward if the correlation isn't significant? Because ordinary regression is sensitive to a handful of points. A few low-budget films with low ratings on the far left (one is rated near 4) pull the left end of the line down. Spearman uses ranks instead of raw values, so it isn't dragged by those few films, and it finds almost no relationship. When the two disagree, the robust one is the safer reading.

What the Pattern Looks Like

Most films sit in a narrow band between roughly 7 and 8.5, whatever they cost. The spread is widest at the low-budget end: some of the cheapest productions are among the best rated, and some are among the worst. Expensive films rarely fall below 7, but they don't reach higher than the best cheap films either. The most-voted films (yellow and green dots) cluster at the expensive end, which reflects marketing reach more than quality.

Limitations

  • Restricted range: only popular films with 1,000+ votes are included, so ratings are compressed near the top of the scale. With so little variation in ratings, any relationship is hard to detect; this chart cannot speak for films in general.
  • Sample size: n = 76. Enough to rule out a strong relationship, not to rule out a small one.
  • Marketing spend: production budgets exclude marketing, which for major releases can match or exceed the production cost.
  • Inflation adjustment has limits: CPI tracks general consumer prices, not film-production costs (star salaries and VFX have grown faster), so older budgets are still somewhat understated in real terms.
  • Rating bias: TMDB ratings come from self-selected users, and older films tend to be rated by people who sought them out.

Conclusion

Among popular films, a bigger budget does not reliably buy a better rating. The rank correlation is weak and not statistically significant, and budget explains under 9% of the variation in ratings. What money does seem to buy is consistency: expensive films rarely flop with audiences, while cheap films produce both the surprises and the disasters.

Which Genre Makes the Most Money? Less Clear Than It Looks

Data source: the 20 most-voted films per genre via TMDB. Only films with reported budget > $5M and box office revenue > $1M are included. ROI = (revenue − budget) / budget × 100. Metric: median ROI per genre, with a 95% percentile-bootstrap confidence interval (2,000 resamples).

What Is ROI?

Return on Investment measures how much a film earns relative to what it cost. A film with a $10M budget that earns $80M has an ROI of 700%; a $200M film that earns $220M has an ROI of only 10%. ROI shows which genres are efficient businesses, something raw box office hides.

How to Read This Chart

  • Bars are the median ROI of each genre's films. The median is used instead of the mean so one runaway hit can't inflate a whole genre.
  • Whiskers are 95% bootstrap confidence intervals: the range where the median would plausibly fall with a different set of films. Wide whiskers mean an unstable median.
  • n= under each genre is the number of films with usable budget and revenue.

The Main Finding: Every Interval Overlaps

Every genre has a positive median ROI, and Thriller has the highest median, followed by Romance (691%) and Comedy (665%). But the confidence intervals of all ten genres overlap. Romance's interval runs from 299% to 1,026%, Comedy's from 395% to 1,245%, and the lower-ranked genres sit inside those same ranges. Statistically, this data cannot say which genre is the most profitable; the ranking from left to right could easily reshuffle with a different sample of films.

The More Interesting Signal: Risk

The width of the whiskers says more than the bar heights. Sci-Fi (354%–587%), Fantasy (339%–624%) and Action (383%–681%) have the narrowest intervals: their top films return a moderate, predictable multiple of their budget. Horror has one of the widest intervals: its films range from modest returns to enormous ones, so its median is unstable. In business terms, big-budget spectacle genres behave like lower-variance bets, while horror is hit-driven.

Documentary looks competitive (median 562%), but it rests on only 5 films and its interval runs from 335% to 3,607%. With that little data the bar is close to meaningless; it is shown for completeness, not as evidence.

Important Limitations

  • Selection bias. Most-voted films are, almost by definition, the ones that found an audience. Flops are under-represented, which is why every genre's median is positive. These are the returns of each genre's hits, not of a typical film.
  • Small samples. At most 20 films per genre (5 for Documentary), as the intervals make visible.
  • Marketing costs are excluded. Production budget and marketing budget are separate; major releases often spend a large share of the production budget again on marketing.
  • Theatrical revenue only. Streaming deals, home media and merchandise (important for Animation and Family titles) never appear in box office figures.
  • Crowd-sourced financials. TMDB budget and revenue figures are self-reported or user-contributed, and many films have none.
  • Genre overlap. A film tagged Horror and Thriller is counted in both genres if it ranks among each one's most-voted films, so the genre samples are not independent.

Conclusion

A bar chart alone would suggest a clear ranking, with Thriller, Romance and Comedy on top. The confidence intervals show that ranking isn't supported by the data: every genre's hits return several times their budget, and the differences between genres are within the noise. The clearer lesson is about risk, not return: big-budget spectacle genres (Sci-Fi, Fantasy, Action) deliver steadier multiples, while genres like Horror are defined by a few outsized successes.
